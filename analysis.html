Co-mention & Geographic Analysis

Social and Spatial Networks in The Electrical Age
1926–1986 • 72,688 location records • 20,037 person records • 10,423 individuals

The following analysis examines which individuals were mentioned together across the pages and articles of The Electrical Age, a magazine published by the Electrical Association for Women. Co-mention relationships — two or more people appearing on the same page or within the same article — offer a window into the professional and social networks visible in the publication. The analysis draws on records spanning 1936 to 1982, covering 2,716 unique pages and 3,807 unique articles.

I

Structural Overview

The first step is to understand the basic shape of the co-mention network: how often do pairs appear together, which individuals are most central, and how large are the groups within which co-mentions occur. These structural features establish the baseline against which all subsequent analyses are read.

Fig. 1.1 — Co-mention count distributions. The vast majority of pairs appear together only once or twice, producing a heavily right-skewed distribution at both page and article level. The percentage of single-occurrence pairs indicates how much of the network is incidental rather than recurring.
Fig. 1.2 — Average group size at co-mention. For each pair, this shows the average number of people present in the groups where they co-occurred. Pairs with a low average group size appeared together in focused, small-group contexts; those with a high average were typically caught up in large roundup articles or contents pages.
Fig. 1.3 — Most central individuals by weighted co-mention score. Each person's score is the sum of co-mention counts across all their pairs — a measure of how much co-mention activity they generate overall. Individuals near the top of this chart are not simply mentioned frequently in isolation; they consistently appear alongside others, making them structurally important to the network.
Fig. 1.4 — Most frequently co-mentioned pairs. The twenty pairs with the highest co-mention counts at both page and article level. Recurring pairs suggest a sustained editorial association — two people who the magazine repeatedly treated as connected, whether through shared roles, joint authorship, or organisational affiliation.
The structural picture is typical of real-world mention networks: a small core of highly connected individuals accounts for a disproportionate share of all co-mention activity, while the majority of pairs are weakly connected and appear together only once.

II

Co-mention Exclusivity

Not all co-mentions are equally meaningful. Two individuals who appear together repeatedly in small, focused articles represent a stronger editorial association than two individuals who happen to share a crowded contents page once. An exclusivity score was calculated for each pair by combining three factors: frequency of co-mention, average group size (smaller is more exclusive), and the number of years over which co-mentions are spread (narrower is more focused). Pairs scoring highly on all three dimensions are those the magazine treated as most consistently and specifically connected.

Fig. 2.1 — Most exclusive co-mentioned pairs. Pairs ranked by exclusivity score, combining frequency, group tightness, and temporal focus. These are the relationships most likely to reflect genuine editorial or professional association rather than coincidental co-presence.
Fig. 2.2 — Frequency vs. group size, coloured by exclusivity. Each point is a co-mentioned pair. The horizontal axis shows how often they appear together; the vertical axis shows the average size of the group they appear in. Green points (high exclusivity) cluster towards high frequency and low group size — pairs that are mentioned together often, and in small company.
The exclusivity analysis separates pairs who are genuinely and repeatedly associated in the editorial record from those whose co-mentions are an artefact of large, catch-all articles. The former are the more historically significant relationships.

IV

Gender Analysis

As a magazine produced by and largely for women working in electrical engineering and allied fields, The Electrical Age offers a distinctive perspective on gender in professional networks. The following charts examine the balance of same-gender and mixed-gender co-mentions, both overall and over time, as well as whether different gender combinations tend to appear in different types of group context.

Fig. 4.1 — Co-mention volume by gender combination. Total co-mention counts broken down by the gender of the two individuals in each pair. This shows the overall balance of same-gender and cross-gender co-mentions in the publication.
Fig. 4.2 — Unique pairs by gender combination. The number of distinct co-mentioned pairs for each gender combination. Comparing this with Fig. 4.1 shows whether certain gender combinations produce a few high-frequency pairs or many low-frequency ones.
Fig. 4.3 — Group size by gender combination. Boxplots showing the distribution of average group size for each gender combination. If mixed-gender pairs tend to appear in larger groups than same-gender pairs, this may suggest that cross-gender co-mentions are more often incidental — occurring in large gatherings — than purposeful.
Fig. 4.4 — Gender proportion of co-mentions per year. Stacked bars showing the proportional share of each gender combination in each year's co-mention activity. Shifts over time may reflect changes in editorial focus, the magazine's readership, or the wider landscape of women's participation in the field.
Fig. 4.5 — F↔F, F↔M and M↔M counts over time. Absolute co-mention counts for the three principal gender combinations plotted as lines. Unlike the proportional view, this shows whether the total volume of each combination grew, shrank, or fluctuated across the decades covered by the dataset.
Gender is not uniformly distributed across article contexts. Examining both proportion and absolute counts together reveals whether the magazine's treatment of male and female professionals changed meaningfully across the decades, or remained structurally stable despite surface variation.

V

Role & Context Analysis

Each individual in the dataset is associated with one or more professional roles — author, editor, engineer, president of a society, government adviser, and so on. This section examines which role combinations appear most frequently in co-mentioned pairs, and which individuals connect across the widest range of professional roles. The latter are of particular historical interest: people who occupied bridge positions between different professional communities are often the most influential actors in a network, even if they are not always the most frequently mentioned.

Fig. 5.1 — Top 20 role combinations in co-mentioned pairs. The most common pairings of professional roles at both page and article level. Dominant role combinations reflect the editorial priorities of the magazine — which professional communities it most often brought into the same frame.
Fig. 5.2 — Individuals who bridge the most distinct roles. Ranked by the number of distinct professional roles represented among their co-mention partners. High scores indicate individuals who appear alongside people from many different professional backgrounds — connectors rather than specialists.
Role diversity among co-mention partners is a useful proxy for institutional influence. Individuals who consistently appear alongside people from a wide range of roles are likely to have occupied central positions in the professional networks the magazine documented.

VI

Ego Network — Caroline Haslett

Caroline Haslett (1895–1957) was the founding secretary and later director of the Electrical Association for Women, editor of The Electrical Age, and one of the most prominent women in British engineering in the mid-twentieth century. Her centrality in the dataset makes her a natural focus for an ego network analysis — a close-up view of one individual's co-mention relationships, across partners, gender, time, and organisational context.

Fig. 6.1 — Top 25 co-mention partners of Caroline Haslett. The individuals most frequently mentioned alongside Haslett in the same article, coloured by gender combination. The ranking reveals her closest editorial associates and the degree to which her network was gender-mixed or gender-homogeneous.
Fig. 6.2 — Haslett co-mention activity over time. Total co-mention count per year, showing when Haslett's presence in the magazine was most concentrated and how her editorial visibility changed across the decades.
Fig. 6.3 — Haslett co-mentions by gender combination over time. Stacked bars showing whether her co-mentions were predominantly with other women, with men, or mixed — and how that balance shifted over time.
Fig. 6.4 — Organisations represented by Haslett's co-mention partners. The organisations most commonly associated with individuals who appear alongside Haslett. This maps the institutional landscape of her editorial network — which bodies and associations she was most consistently connected to through shared article space.
Haslett's ego network illustrates in miniature the broader patterns of the dataset: a core of close, recurring associates surrounded by a wider constellation of occasional co-mentions, spanning multiple organisations and — notably for the period — a considerable range of professional roles on both sides of the gender divide.

VII

Article Type Breakdown

A co-mention in an advertisement carries different historical weight from one in a feature article or an obituary. This section classifies articles by type — feature, advertisement, editorial, report, obituary, and so on — and examines how co-mention patterns differ across those categories. The results reveal which article types drove the most co-mention activity, and whether gender dynamics varied by context.

Fig. 7.1 — Co-mention volume and unique pairs by article type. Left panel: total co-mention count by article type. Right panel: number of unique co-mentioned pairs by article type. Together these show which article types produced the most co-mention activity and whether that activity was concentrated in a few recurring pairs or spread across many.
Fig. 7.2 — Gender proportion by article type. Whether the gender balance of co-mentions differs across article types. For instance, obituaries and feature articles may show a higher proportion of F↔F pairs than advertisements, which tend to reference public figures regardless of gender.
Fig. 7.3 — Group size by article type. Boxplots of average group size for each article type. Contents pages and reports tend to mention many people at once; obituaries and profiles tend to focus on one or two. This contextualises the co-mention counts in Figs. 7.1 and 7.2.
Article type is a significant moderating variable. Co-mentions that occur in feature articles and obituaries — where editorial choices about who to name are deliberate — are likely to be more historically meaningful than those arising in advertisements or contents listings.

VIII

Depiction vs. Mention

The dataset distinguishes between individuals who are merely mentioned in an article and those who are also depicted — photographed or illustrated. Being depicted alongside another person is a stronger signal of editorial association than a passing textual reference. This section examines how the depiction dimension intersects with the co-mention network: which pairs involve at least one depicted individual, who was depicted most often, and whether depicted individuals skew towards any particular gender.

Fig. 8.1 — Co-mention count distribution: depicted vs. mentioned only. Log-scale histogram comparing the frequency distribution of co-mention counts for pairs where at least one person was depicted against pairs where neither was. Depicted pairs tend to cluster at higher co-mention counts, consistent with the idea that depicted individuals are more editorially prominent.
Fig. 8.2 — Top 20 most frequently depicted individuals. Individuals ranked by the number of times they appear as depicted across the dataset. Repeated depiction signals sustained editorial prominence — these are the faces the magazine chose to put before its readers most often.
Fig. 8.3 — Gender breakdown of depicted individuals. Whether the gender balance of depicted individuals matches the overall gender balance in the dataset, or whether the magazine disproportionately chose to depict individuals of one gender.
Depiction is a form of editorial emphasis. Pairs where at least one member was depicted represent the most visually prominent co-mention relationships in the magazine — those the editors considered worthy of a face, not just a name.

IX

Temporal Patterns

The dataset spans nearly five decades, from the mid-1930s to the early 1980s, though coverage is uneven across years. The temporal analysis examines how co-mention activity varied over time, how long co-mentioned pairs persisted across years, and what proportion of each year's co-mention activity consisted of new relationships versus the return of established ones. These patterns speak to the stability — or turnover — of the professional networks the magazine documented.

Fig. 9.1 — Co-mention activity per year. Total co-mention counts at page and article level for each year in the dataset. Peaks and troughs may reflect both genuine editorial patterns and the uneven coverage of the digitised archive.
Fig. 9.2 — Pair longevity: how many years do pairs span? For each co-mentioned pair, the number of distinct years in which they appeared together. Most pairs are short-lived; a small number persist across many years, suggesting long-term editorial association.
Fig. 9.3 — New vs. returning co-mentioned pairs per year. For each year, the blue portion shows pairs appearing together for the first time in the dataset; the pink portion shows pairs that had co-occurred in at least one earlier year. A high proportion of returning pairs in a given year indicates a stable, recurring network; a high proportion of new pairs indicates expansion or turnover.
The balance of new and returning pairs over time is one of the most informative temporal signals in the dataset. Years dominated by returning pairs point to a settled editorial community; years with many new pairs suggest the magazine was either expanding its coverage or responding to external change — the outbreak of war, for instance, or shifts in the profession itself.

X

Geographic Overview

Transitioning from social networks to spatial coverage, this section maps the reach of The Electrical Age across 72,688 records from 1926 to 1986. The dataset tracks 8,045 distinct locations across 289 countries, revealing how the magazine served as a bridge between local branches and the global electrical industry.

Fig. 10.1 — Top 20 most-mentioned standardised locations. London stands as the primary hub of activity with 5,799 mentions. The distribution highlights major industrial centers like Birmingham, Manchester, and Glasgow as secondary nodes in the magazine's geographic network.
The record is overwhelmingly UK-centric, with the United Kingdom accounting for 86.56% of all mentions across over 6,000 distinct locations.
